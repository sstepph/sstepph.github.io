Research Focus

How well do fine-tuned machine translation models translate Syrian Arabic into English? Most MT systems are built around Modern Standard Arabic (MSA), while everyday communication happens in dialects. We fine-tune and compare five sequence-to-sequence models, then look closely at two hard cases: dialect-specific homonyms and semantically loaded named entities (names and places that also have a literal meaning). We also test whether adding an Arabic NER model to the pipeline helps.

Background

Arab countries live in a situation of diglossia: MSA is the "high" language of education and formal writing, while the local dialect is the "low" language of daily life. Some dialects differ so much from MSA that they are not mutually intelligible, and very little data exists for them.

Phonological variation

Some vowels and consonants shift in spoken Levantine Arabic. For example, /θ/ (ث) tends to become /t/.

Morphological variation

The future marker: سـ /sa/ or سوف /sawfa/ in MSA, but حـ /ħ/ or رح /raħ/ in Levantine dialects.

Lexical variation

"Tomorrow" is غداً /ɣadan/ in MSA, but بكرا /bukra/ in Levantine dialects.

Orthographic variation

MSA has a standard spelling. Dialects are not standardized, so spelling varies from writer to writer.

Previous Work on Dialectal Arabic MT

2006
Statistical segmentation (Riesa & Yarowsky)

Statistical segmentation outperformed rule-based approaches for Iraqi and Levantine Arabic.

2010
Pivot through MSA (Sawaf)

A hybrid rule-based + statistical system normalized dialects into MSA before translation, with modest BLEU gains.

2011
Morphological mapping & crowdsourcing

Salloum & Habash mapped dialectal morphemes to MSA to reduce out-of-vocabulary rates. Zaidan & Callison-Burch used Amazon Mechanical Turk to build cost-effective parallel corpora.

2012
Direct dialect-to-English translation (Zbib et al.)

A 1.5M-word Egyptian and Levantine corpus enabled a phrase-based hierarchical system that beat MSA-based systems by 6.3 to 7 BLEU points.

Pre-processing Pipeline

1. Cleaning

Trim extra spaces, remove special and foreign characters, diacritics (Syrian side) and contractions (English side).

2. Normalization

Normalize the various forms of a letter in Syrian Arabic.

3. Splitting

80% training, 10% validation, 10% testing.

4. Tokenization

Each model's own tokenizer, mainly SentencePiece and Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE).

NER-Augmented Translation Pipeline

1
Detect

ANER finds named entities. Only those with confidence above 85% are kept.

2
Replace

Each entity is swapped for its tag (e.g. PERSON, LOCATION).

3
Translate

The tagged sentence is translated into English.

4
Reinsert

The original entities are put back in place of the tags.

Why proper nouns are tricky

Many Arabic names and places have a literal meaning. They should be transliterated, not translated.

Discussion & Conclusion

Key findings

  • Fine-tuning, even on a small dataset, clearly improves dialect-specific translation.
  • NLLB-200 and M2M-100 were the strongest models (BLEU 36.23%, ROUGE above 0.6).
  • AraT5v2 performed worst, suggesting familiarity with English is essential.
  • The NER integration had no significant impact on NLLB-200, M2M-100 and MarianMT.
  • Llama 3.1 8B handled proper nouns best, so more LLMs are worth exploring.

Limitations

  • Arabic dialects are low-resource: a small corpus covering few themes.
  • Many out-of-vocabulary words the models could not translate.
  • Manual evaluation was done by native Syrian Arabic speakers bilingual in English, not native English speakers.
  • No standard transliteration for Arabic names.

Ethical concerns

  • Prioritizing one dialect can marginalize others, and a single "Syrian dialect" erases variation between cities and neighborhoods.
  • Standardizing an oral language risks imposing an arbitrary written norm that misses its linguistic and cultural richness.
  • Linking dialects to English can reinforce linguistic imperialism, where a dialect's value is measured by its link to a global lingua franca.
